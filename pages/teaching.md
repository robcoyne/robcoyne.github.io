## Classroom Teaching

**PHY-49X: General Relativity** This course is targeted at advanced undergraduates and junior graduate students who have not yet taken a course in General Relativity. It begins with a review of special relativity, and subsequently establishes the basics of tensor algebra and tensor calculus. From there, we derive the Einstein field equations and explore specific solutions thereof (i.e. Schwarzschild, Reissner–Nordström, and Kerr black holes, gravitational waves, and others as time allows).

**PHY-306: Elementary Modern Physics** This course is typically taken by physics majors in the second semester of their sophomore year, or by students of other majors who have completed the introductory sequence of physics courses and are pursuing a minor in physics. The course covers introductions to special relativity (time dilation, length contraction, simultaneity, and the Lorentz Transformations), quantum mechanics (wave-particle duality, uncertainty, the Schrödinger equation, and solutions thereof up to – and including – the radial component of the Hydrogen atom), and particle physics (a survey of elementary particles, the physics that govern them, and the Standard Model). 

**PHY-205: Elementary Physics III** The third course in the introductory physics sequence covers fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, wave behavior, and geometric optics. The lecture component of the course covers base principles, and explores their implications through active learning exercises. The laboratory component includes experimental applications of the theory covered in lecture. And the recitation component includes targeted group-based problem solving exercises. The honors version of the course extends each of these components by incorporating deeper explorations of the theory, and the development of additional "soft skills" like scientific writing and presentations.

**PHY-204: Elementary Physics II** The second course in the introductory physics sequence covers electricity, magnetism, and ciruit theory applications. The course follows a pattern of theory, followed by application. The physics of Electric fields (and their applications) are then followed by simple circuit applications involving capacitors, resistance, and current. Then the physics of Magnetic fields (and their applications) are followed by the introduction of inductors and time-dependent circuits. Notably, in addition to being a required course for physics majors, successful completion of this course is a requirement for admission into URI's college of engineering.

## Teaching Outside of the Classroom

**How to Give Great Presentations: A scientist's guide to effective communication** I co-developed a one-day workshop on scientific communication that has run annually as a part of the regular meetings of the American Astronomical Society, starting with the 240th meeting in June 2022. This workshop covers the design and delivery of both oral and poster presentations, with advice for in-person, hybrid, and virtual formats. Participants also get opportunities to develop and practice their own presentations, with advice from the organizers and guest panelists who have been recognized for their excellence in scientific communication.

**The LSC's Communication and Education Division** I have been an active participant in the LIGO Scientific Collaboration's Communication and Education division (responsible for EPO – education and public outreach) since shortly after I joined the collaboration in 2013, including serving as its chair. In this capacity I have helped develop and maintain numerous education products (both formal and informal) across a wide range of collaboration activities.
